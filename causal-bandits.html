Causal Bandits

Notes on learning good interventions via causal inference in bandit problems from Tor Lattimore et al

certainty: importance: 8

Background

Drug testing, policy setting can be framed as Bandit Problems. Single actions, or Interventions from a pre-determined set are repeatedly performed in order to evaluate their effectiveness via feedback from a single, real-valued reward signal.

The authors generalize this standard setting further by assuming that in addition to the reward signal, the learner observes the values of a drawn from a Probabilitic Causal Model
(Causal models are commonly used in disciplines where explicit experimentation is difficult – social science, demography, et cetera – such as when predicting the effect of changes of childcare subsidies \(\rightarrow\) workforce participation, or choice of schools \(\rightarrow\) grades).

This is illustrated through an example – a farmer wishes to optimise the yield of her crop. The crop yield is affected by {temperature, soil nutrient, moisture}, only. He has a particular cost to Intervene and adjust at most one of these variables (I suppose these are the mentioned Covariates?). There is also a general change in the variables at the end of every season, that is observed at the end, along with the final crop yield. How is the farmer to best experiment to identify the single, highest yielding intervention in a limited number of seasons?

These Interventions are treated as Arms in a Bandit problem, and their influence on the reward, is assumed to conform to some known causal graph – this seems to be the primary contribution of the paper, the addition of a causal graph. This subsumes and generalizes the classical bandits framework which admits no additional observations, and contextual stochastic bandit problems – where the observations are revealed before an intervention is chosen. In the most general case, as presented here, the observations occur after the Intervention. Interesting that the case where observations come before are also subsumed by the case where observations come later.

We shall define Regret as the difference between the return of the optimal action and the action chosen by the algorithm after \(T\) rounds.

Relation to Previous Algorithms

Causal Bandit Problems \(\rightarrow\) Classical Multi-Armed Bandit + Causal Model and some extra observations. Gaurav suggests this isn’t the right way to look at the problem.

The classical multi-armed problem has existing algorithms. However, it is possible to show (and is intuitive), that ignoring the extra information is suboptimal.

The Contextual Bandits problem does have side-information, and there is a similarity to Contextual Bandits, since extra observations on non-intervened variables (why only the Non-Intervened Variables? Probably because I can always see the effect of my Intervention anyway, it’s the side information on the change of the crop yield by the weather that’s new to me – albeit that it’s observed only at the end) may be viewed as context. However, importantly – we don’t have this context before the Intervention, so cannot be Context. It’s side-information nonetheless.

Difference between Cumulative Regret and Simple Regret is important – there are other papers that bring causal inference to Bandit problems – in the presence of Confounding Variables(?) the value that a variable would have taken had it not been intervened can provide important contextual information. Here, however, the focus is on the Cumulative Regret, and the Context is observed before the action is taken and cannot be controlled by the learning agent.

Problem Formalization

They introduce a class of a Stochastic Decision Problems, that they call the Causal Bandit Problem.

The model parameters are the following – A directed acyclic graph \(\mathcal{G}\) over a Set of Random Variables \(\mathcal{X} = \{X_1, \dots, X_N\}\) and a Joint Distribution \(P\) over \(\mathcal{X}\) factorises (what?) over \(\mathcal{G}\).

Assumptions: Each variable only takes on a finite number of distinct values.

An edge from variable \(X_i\) to \(X_j\) means that changes in value of \(X_i\) may cause a change in the value of \(X_j\) – along those lines, the parents of \(X_i\) are the subset \(\mathcal{P}_{a_{X_i}}\) of \(\mathcal{X}\), whose edges terminate on \(X_j\).

do(\(\textbf{X} = \textbf{x}\)) assigns the value \(\textbf{x} = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}\) to corresponding variables \(\textbf{X} = \{X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\}\). The empty intervention is denoted by \(do()\).

Why does the following happen? The \(do(\textbf{X} = \textbf{x})\) action prunes the Causal Graph such that the elements \(X_i \in \textbf{X}\) are removed from \(\mathcal{P}a_{X_i}\). The resulting graph will define a Probability Distribution \(P \{X^c | \textit{do(\textbf{X = x}})\}\) where \(\textbf{X}^c\) is the complement of the set \(\textbf{X}\) passed as an argument to \(do()\).

A Learner has access to the Causal Graph \(\mathcal{G}\) and a set of allowed actions, \(\mathcal{A}\). In addition, one of the variables in \(\mathcal{X}\) is designated as \(Y\), the Reward Variable, taking on values in \(\{0, 1\}\) (this seems incompatible with the farmer example – do the crops only wilt or survive? Can we extend this to real rewards?).

The expected reward for the action \(a = do(\textbf{X} = \textbf{x})\) is given by \(\mu_a = \mathbb{E}[Y | do(\textbf{X} = \textbf{x})]\). The optimal expected reward by \(\mu^* := \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mu_a\).

The Causal Bandit Game proceeds over \(T\) rounds; at round \(t\), the learner intervenes by choosing \(a_t = do(\textbf{X}_t = \textbf{x}_t) \in \mathcal{A})\), based on some previous observations. In addition: all Non-Intervened variables \(X_t^c\) drawn from \(P\{X_t^c|do(\textbf{X}_t = \textbf{x}_t)\}\), including the reward, \(Y_t \in \{0, 1\}\).

The Aim: After \(T\) observations, the Learner should output an estimate of the optimal action \(\hat{a}^*_T \in \mathcal{A}\) based on the prior observations.

The Objective: Minimize the Simple Regret: \(R_T = \mu^* - \mathbb{E}[\mu_{\hat{a}_T}\), also called pure exploration, or best-arm identification, where the idea is that a policy will be fixed indefinitely.

We recognize that this structure allows us to recover past frameworks by adjusting the order of information and/or the underlying causal graph.

Analysis: Parallel Bandit Problem

We set up the problem as follows: we have N binary variables \(\{X_1, \dots X_N\}\) where each \(X_i \in \{0, 1\}\) are Independent Causes Hence the Parallel in parallel bandits? of a reward variable \(Y \in \{0, 1\}\).

(This also generalizes the farming example – \(X_1 = 0\) or \(1\) may mean the temperature is high, or low, and so on.)

Further, the Learner either purely observes by \(do()\) (do nothing), or sets the value of a single variable. The remaining variables (non-intervened) are set by independently-biased coin flips, from a Bernoulli Distribution, so \(X_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(q_i)\) where \(\textbf{q} = (q_1, \dots, q_N) \in [0, 1]^N\).

This is important: the Reward Variable is distributed as \(P\{Y = 1|\textbf{X}\} = r(\textbf{X})\) – note that the distribution of the reward is dependent on all covariates at that step, as it should be, where \(r : \{0, 1\}^N \rightarrow [0, 1]\) is an arbitrary, fixed, unknown function. Can we add an Oracle to this formalization? For example, a farmer who knows some qualities of the function – in a non-binary case, for example, too much heat is bad. A non-binary case could always be binarized.

The dependence of the rewards on the variables, due to the nature of the fact that this is a distribution, is stochastic. Causal Graphs seem to be constrained to be DAGs.
Algorithm: The algorithm described in the paper is,

  1. For the first \(\frac{T}{2}\) rounds, choose \(do()\), and collect observational data.

  2. As the only link (as it’s a Parallel Bandit), it follows that if \(P{Y|X_i = j}\) is large, we’ll get good estimates via \(do(X_i = j)\) , and for small values, we might not observe anything, so the estimates of their returns might be poor Can we estimate how poor?.

  3. To address the aforementioned problem, Split remaining rounds between infrequently observed actions.

Natural question – How infrequent is bad enough? Refresher – vector \(\textbf{q}\) defines the Bernoulli parameter that tunes how often something comes up – we want to answer how many of the variables are unbalanced? Intuitively, this arises from how small \(q_i\) or \((1-q_i)\) is.

The following statement is possibly the most confusing part of the paper.
For indices \(I_{\tau} = \{i: \min\{q_i, 1 - q_i\} < \frac{1}{\tau}\}\). We define \(m(\textbf{q}) = \min\{\tau : |I_{\tau} \leq \tau\}\).

Aside: Let’s compute a few cases of \(m(\textbf{q})\) to clear this out. If \(\textbf{q} = (\frac{1}{2}, \dots, \frac{1}{2})\), then \(m(\textbf{q}) = \min\{\tau \text{ such that } |I_\tau| \leq \tau\}\). We can compute the possible values of \(\tau\) here and take the minimum, and \(\tau \in \mathbb{Z} : 2 \leq \tau \leq N\), where \(N = |\textbf{q}|\).

The value of \(\min\{q_i, 1 - q_i\}\) is identically \(\frac{1}{2}\) here. So, \(I_{\tau} = {i : \min\{q_i, 1 - q_i\} < \frac{1}{\tau}\}}\) is \(0\) for all \(\tau \geq 2\), and \(N\) for \(\tau = 1\), since all \(\forall q_i \in q, q_i \leq \frac{1}{1}\). So, we want to see the minimum value of \(\tau\), which is, here, \(2\).

By similar arguments, if \(\textbf{q} = (0, \dots, 0)\), \(\min\{q_i, 1-q_i\}\) is identically 0. So, every value of \(\tau\) produces a set of size \(N\), and so, we must choose \(N\). Run some experiments in python and see how this function varies, perhaps?

From some toying around, it seems like \(m(\textbf{q})\) is the best case – possible when all are perfectly balanced (you get maximum exploration by doing nothing), but when all values are close to \(1\) or \(0\), we have \(m(\textbf{q}) = N\) (which means you have seen only one side of the coin flip every time). Essentially, High \(m(\textbf{q})\) means that our observations have accounted to very little, and there’s a lot to see. The converse is also true.

The paper’s primary claim is that the usage of the Causal Structure allows the Algorithm to only explore the \(m(\textbf{q})\) difficult actions. Difficult can perhaps more accurately be described as Rare. Perhaps because real-life systems are highly stochastic (useful to note here that \(m(\textbf{q})\) is high when the Shannon Entropy seems to be low), values of \(m(\textbf{q})\) is low. Interestingly, it may directly imply that there isn’t much information present in the system, and hence many steps are required to exploit the system. What does it even mean to calculate the entropy of a set of probability distribution parameters?

Analysis: Regret Bounds for General Graphs

Consider the more general problem – the graph structure is . By the Causal Graph, Correlation \(\neq\) Causation \(\implies\) \(P\{Y | X_i = j\} \neq P\{Y | do(X_i = j)\}\). Note the Causal Graph above for reference.

!**\(do(X = j)\) affects only \(Y\) (Causation, but if \(X\) happens spontaneously, it is associated with changes in both \(Z\) and \(Y\) – Correlation** is bidirectional.

note to self: Book of Why, Judea Pearl.

The following Truncated Factorization Formula is attributed to Judea Pearl, father of Causal Inference and Do-Calculus. It represents the result of a conditioning with a \(do()\) operation without a \(do()\) operation. \[P\{X_1, \dots, X_N \ do(X_i = j)\} = \prod_{k \neq i} P\{X_k | \mathcal{P}a_{X_k}\} \delta(X_i - j),\] where \(\mathcal{P}a_{X_k}\), as before, denotes the parents of \(X_k\), \(\delta\) is just the Dirac Delta function.

Note that naïvely using the same algorithm as Parallel Bandits from before will not work here. Here’s how to do it – we write each \(P\{Y|a\}\) in terms of observational quantities, and then explicitly play actions for which the Observed Quantities are poor.

The primary difference here is that the general Causal Graph, while compulsorily a DAG, can still possess nodes that have edges directed towards them – something we disregarded in the Parallel Bandits case. What this does is that it ruins our assumption that we can \(do()\) until we have a sufficiently strong estimator of at least the common events, and then move on to the rare(r) variables. Here, a \(do(X_i = j)\) does not induce the same changes in the target \(Y\) as the case where we \(do()\) and independently observe \(X_i = j\).

Assumptions: We assume we know the Conditional Interventional Distributions \(P\{\mathcal{P}a_{Y} | a\}\) but not \(P\{Y|a\}\). This has a physical meaning. Consider the following Causal Graph. Imagine that Crop Yield is caused directly by Soil Moisture, and so, Soil Moisture \(\in \mathcal{P}a_Y\). However, Irrigation causes Soil Moisture, and so it indirectly causes the Crop Yield. What we are assuming we know here is how the factors in our control are affected by other factors that are also in our control – in other words, we don’t assume knowledge of how \(Y\), or Crop Yield changes with Soil Moisture, or (considering we control it), the Weather, but we do know how Weather and Irrigation affect the Soil Moisture. Note that we need only assume we know how the set \(\mathcal{P}a_Y\), consisting of Soil Moisture, Nutrient Level, and Weather change in this respect – we don’t assume knowledge about how something can affect Irrigation – we need not know this to arrive at an algorithm.

Estimating the conditional distributions for all variables in an optimal way is a research problem. Check if it has been solved.

Now, let \(\eta\) be a distribution on the available interventions \(a \in \mathcal{A}\), so \(\eta_a \geq 0\) and \(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}} = 1.\) Also, define the following Mixture distribution: \(Q = \sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\eta_{a} P\{\mathcal{P}a_Y | a\}\) over the interventions with respect to \(\eta\).

Note: Mixture distributions are also trivially still distributions, as we can move the integral inside the summand – very roughly, the idea is that \(\int_{i} w_i P_i dx\) is the mixture, \(\iint_{i} w_i P_i dx dx\) is the integral of the PDF. \(w_i\) is independent of \(P_i\), so the integral can be moved inward, \(\int_i w_i (\int_i P_i dx) dx\) = \(\int_i w_i (1) dx = 1\). Here, \(P\{\mathcal{P}a_Y | a\}\) weighs each action by how likely it is to affect the parents of \(Y\) – does this measure how often it turns \(P\)

For example, in the example we took, we’d have \(P\{\mathcal{P}a_Y | do(A: \text{\text{Irrigation}})\}\) be, say \(M\), a mapping from \(\{0, 1\}^3 \rightarrow [0, 1] \in \mathbb{R}\), which tells us the resultant probability distribution over {Soil Moisture, Nutrient Level, Weather} when Irrigation is performed. Now, we weight each of \(M_i\) with \(\eta_a\) for the given action – the probability that we are going to \(do(\text{\text{Irrigation}})\).

The proposed algorithm samples \(T\) actions from our distribution over actions, \(\eta\), and uses them to estimate the returns \(\mu_a\) for all \(a \in \mathcal{A}\) simultaneously via Truncated Importance Weighted Estimator. We let the \(\mathcal{P}a_{Y}(X)\) denote the realization of variables \(X\) that are in the set \(\mathcal{P}a_Y\). We define the function \(R_a(X) = \frac{P\{\mathcal{P}a_Y(X)|a\}}{Q\{\mathcal{P}a_Y(X)\}}\). Let us look at the numerator and denominator more closely. Also, note that \(X\) here, unbolded, is a set of concrete realizations of variables \(\textbf{x} \in \textbf{X}\)

\(P(\mathcal{P}a_Y(X) | a)\) queries the likelihood of observing \(\mathcal{P}a_Y(X)\) after action \(a\) is done (needs confirmation). This can be measured as we know the conditional interventional distribution \(P\{\mathcal{P}a_Y | a\}\).

\(Q\{\mathcal{P}a_Y(X)\}\) uses the aforementioned Mixture Distribution, where we weight the \(P\{\mathcal{P}a_Y | a\}\) (the distribution over the parents of \(Y\) after some action \(a\)) with the probability of choosing that action \(a\) in the first place.

Essentially, given an action \(a\), and a set of concrete realizations \(X\), \(R_a(X)\) is the ratio of the likelihood of observing the concrete values \(X\) among the parents of \(Y\), and the likelihood of \(X\) being realized from our mixture distribution – that is, the chances of us intervening and finding \(X\), based on our own distribution over the actions. This is the Importance Weighted part of the Truncated Importance Weighted Estimator.

Then, the expected return for an action \(a\) is, \[\hat{\mu}_a = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} Y_t R_a(X_t) \mathds{1}{R_a(X_t) \leq B_a}\], where \(B_a \geq 0\) is a constant to tune the degree of truncation. The important part here is that we’re again weighting \(\mu_a\) with the values of \(Y_t\) that are being observed – this is what serves us the information required to choose the optimal answer.

A proof for the Regret Bound on the Parallel Bandits Case

Some preliminaries: First, we write the Hoeffding Bound: \(P(\frac{1}{n}\sum \left(X_t - \mathbb{E}(X_t) \geq \epsilon \right) \leq \exp{\left(\frac{-2n^2\epsilon}{\sum(b-a)^2}\right)}\), where \(b\) and \(a\) are the bounds of the random variable. In our case, \(b = 1, a = 0\), so the denominator vanishes.
